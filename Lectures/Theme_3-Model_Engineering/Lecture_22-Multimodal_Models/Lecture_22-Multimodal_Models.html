This lecture introduces multi-modal models, which process and integrate information from more than one data modality, such as text, images, audio, and video. We will focus on Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which are currently the most widely used class of multi-modal models. We will cover VLM architectures, including modern natively multi-modal models, as well as vision encoders and training objectives of VLMs, followed by an example of finetuning a VLM on a custom dataset of medical images. The lecture concludes with a discussion of VLM benchmarking, the importance and limitations of VLMs, and a brief overview of other types of multi-modal models.

22.1 Introduction to Vision-Language Models

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are multi-modal systems that jointly process and reason over visual information (images, videos) and linguistic information (text). By integrating the two modalities, VLMs learn relationships between visual content and natural language.

Two general families of VLMs are:

  • Embedding-based VLMs (e.g., CLIP) encode images and text into aligned representations. They support similarity scoring, retrieval, and zero-shot classification, but do not themselves generate conversational responses.
  • Generative VLMs (e.g., LLaVA, Qwen-VL) generate text conditioned on visual inputs and a text prompt. These models can describe images, answer questions about them, and hold image-grounded conversations.

The following diagram illustrates the generative type of VLMs.

Figure: VLM structure. Source: [1].

Training data for VLMs can include image-caption pairs, documents with interleaved images and text, visual question-answer pairs, grounding annotations, and video-text examples. Building datasets for VLMs requires collecting a large number of images and corresponding text. Several very large datasets that have been essential for training modern VLMs contain millions or billions of image-text pairs, with descriptions in English and other languages. For instance, the LAION-5B dataset has 5.8 billion image-text examples, and the PMD (Public Multimodal Dataset) contains 70 million image-text examples. However, dataset scale alone does not guarantee a high-quality VLM, as captions may be noisy or contain information that is not visible in the image.

During training, a VLM learns relationships between visual and textual representations, which allow the model to associate visual features (shapes, colors, spatial relations) with linguistic concepts. Different training objectives teach the models different capabilities. For instance, contrastive models learn image-text compatibility, whereas generative models learn to produce text conditioned on visual inputs.

The figure below illustrates a VLM performing tasks such as object localization, segmentation, visual question answering, and learning from image-based instructions. The user prompts are shown on the left, and the model responses are shown on the right. As demonstrated in this example, VLMs can interpret not only the semantic content of images, but also spatial relations in images, such as the relative positions of objects. Some VLMs can also output bounding boxes or segmentation masks of objects, although these capabilities require suitable training data and output formats, and are not supported by every VLM.

Figure: VLM prompts and responses. Source: [4].

In general, VLMs can perform various multi-modal tasks, including:

  • Image and video captioning or summarization: generate context-aware descriptions of images or video frames.
  • Visual question answering (VQA): answer open-ended questions based on visual content.
  • Image-based reasoning and grounding: provide explanations or logical reasoning about visual scenes, or identify the object referred to by a phrase.
  • Text-to-image search: retrieve images, figures, or diagrams in documents that match a textual query.
  • Optical Character Recognition (OCR): read text in images, documents, tables, and charts.
  • Image generation and editing: generate new images or edit existing images based on textual prompts (only in models designed for visual outputs).

22.2 VLM Architectures

Numerous VLM architectures have been proposed in recent years that employ different strategies to integrate visual and textual information. Two broad categories are VLM architectures with shared multi-modal embeddings and VLM architectures with fused multi-modal embeddings, illustrated in the figure.

Figure: VLM architectures. Source: [3].

22.2.1 VLMs with Shared Multi-modal Embeddings

VLMs with shared multi-modal embeddings typically have a dual-encoder architecture (i.e., separate vision and text encoders) that produces aligned embeddings. These models include the following main components [5].

Multi-modal inputs: Input modalities include visual inputs (images, PDF documents, or videos) and textual inputs (captions, question-answer pairs, or instructions).

Encoders: A vision encoder converts an image into visual features, and a text encoder converts the text (e.g., an image caption) into text features. The vision encoder in VLMs is commonly a variant of the Vision Transformer (ViT), although other designs are also possible. The text encoder is typically a Transformer-based encoder that is pretrained on large text corpora.

Projection and alignment: The visual and textual features are projected into a shared multi-modal embedding space. This step is achieved through a projector head (a.k.a. projector layer, or just projector), which is usually implemented as a linear layer or a small block of fully-connected layers. The two encoders process the image and the text separately, and the projectors align their outputs so that the embeddings of an image and its matching description are close to each other in the shared embedding space.

The shared embedding space enables VLMs to link textual concepts (e.g., the word “cat”) with the corresponding visual content (images of cats). For instance, the model represents “cat” not just as a word, but also in terms of how cats appear in images. Note, however, that a shared embedding space supports matching images and text, and it does not by itself provide detailed localization of objects or conversational reasoning.

One representative model of this type of VLM architecture is CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining) [9]. It was one of the first models to introduce large-scale vision-language alignment through contrastive learning. CLIP employs both a vision encoder and a text encoder, and learns a shared embedding space in which images and their textual descriptions are semantically aligned. Contrastive learning in CLIP is employed to associate visual and textual content by maximizing the similarity between matched image and text pairs and minimizing the similarity between mismatched pairs. In the figure below, the image encoder outputs image embeddings \(I_1, I_2, I_3, ..., I_N\) for the images in the batch, and the text encoder outputs text embeddings \(T_1, T_2, T_3, ..., T_N\) for the corresponding texts in the batch. The model computes a similarity score \(I_i\cdot T_j\) between each image embedding (e.g., \(I_i\)) and text embedding (e.g., \(T_j\)) in the batch. The similarity scores are calculated as the dot product of the image and text embeddings, which equals the cosine similarity because CLIP normalizes the embeddings to unit length. As training progresses, the two encoders are updated so that image and text representations corresponding to similar concepts are drawn close to each other in the shared embedding space, while dissimilar concepts are pushed apart.

Figure: CLIP architecture. Source: [2].

A trained CLIP model can perform zero-shot classification: an image is encoded together with candidate text prompts such as “a photo of a cat” and “a photo of a dog”, and the prompt with the most similar embedding is selected as the predicted class. In this case, the set of classes can be changed without retraining the model.

The pretrained vision encoder of CLIP has been widely adopted as the vision encoder component in numerous later VLMs.

22.2.2 VLMs with Fused Multi-modal Embeddings

Generative VLMs commonly employ architectures with fused multi-modal embeddings, which combine a pretrained vision encoder with a pretrained decoder-only language model. These models fuse image and text representations into joint multi-modal representations, which enables more advanced visual reasoning.

Two important connection patterns in this category of VLMs are:

  • Projected visual tokens: a projector maps the visual features to the hidden dimension of the language model. The visual tokens are placed alongside the text-token embeddings, and the language model decoder applies self-attention over the combined sequence of visual and text tokens. LLaVA is an example of this pattern.
  • Cross-attention: the layers of the language model attend to a separate sequence of visual features through cross-attention. Flamingo and Llama 3.2 Vision are examples of this pattern.

VLMs with Fused Embeddings and Projected Visual Tokens

The following figure illustrates the workflow of VLMs with projected visual tokens. The visual embeddings from the vision encoder are first transformed by the multi-modal projector into the embedding space of the LLM. Note that the projector does not match each image token to a corresponding word; instead, it learns a transformation that makes the visual features usable by the LLM. The LLM decoder receives a combined input sequence consisting of visual embeddings (image tokens) from the projector and text embeddings (text tokens) from the user’s prompt. The LLM decoder serves as the text generation backbone, and using the combined image and text tokens, it generates a textual output in an autoregressive manner, one token at a time. Each new token is conditioned on the previously generated tokens and on the image and text tokens in the input.

The original LLaVA model used a single linear layer as the projector, and LLaVA-1.5 replaced it with a two-layer MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron) [10, 11].

Figure: Fused multi-modal VLM with projected visual tokens. Source: [4].

VLMs with Fused Embeddings and Cross-attention

Representative models of this class of VLM architectures (shown on the right in the above figure on VLM architectures) are BLIP and Flamingo, which use a cross-attention mechanism to fuse image and text embeddings [7]. This mechanism is similar to the cross-attention module that connects the encoder and decoder in the standard Transformer network. Instead of adding image tokens to the input sequence, cross-attention layers inserted in the language model allow the text tokens to attend directly to the visual features. Llama 3.2 Vision is a more recent model that also uses cross-attention layers [12].

22.2.3 Modern VLMs

The VLMs discussed above laid the foundations for modern multi-modal models. Among open-weight VLMs, the design with projected visual tokens is now dominant: models such as Qwen3-VL, InternVL, Gemma 3, and Pixtral combine a ViT-based vision encoder, an MLP projector, and an LLM decoder. The improvements in these models mostly concern how images are converted into visual tokens (e.g., support for different image resolutions and aspect ratios, and merging of neighboring visual tokens), as well as the scale and quality of the training data. Cross-attention designs have become less common. For instance, Meta used cross-attention in Llama 3.2 Vision [12], but adopted a different design in Llama 4 [17]. NVIDIA compared the two designs in its NVLM models, and found that the model with projected visual tokens achieved higher accuracy on OCR-related tasks, whereas the cross-attention model was more computationally efficient for processing high-resolution images [18].

Late fusion and early fusion. Multi-modal architectures can also be distinguished by the stage at which the modalities are fused:

  • Late fusion: the image is first processed by a separate, deep vision encoder, which is typically pretrained on its own (e.g., the vision encoder of CLIP), and the output of the encoder is then fused with the text in the language model. The VLMs with projected visual tokens and with cross-attention that we discussed above are late-fusion models.
  • Early fusion: the image is converted into tokens with minimal processing, and the image and text tokens are processed together by a single Transformer from the first layer. For example, Fuyu has no image encoder, and the image patches are linearly projected into the first layer of the Transformer [19], while Chameleon converts images into discrete image tokens, similar to text tokens, which allows it to both understand and generate images [20].

Figure: Late-fusion and early-fusion VLM architectures.

Natively multi-modal models. A related distinction concerns how the models are trained. Most open-weight VLMs are built by connecting a vision encoder to an LLM that was first pretrained on text only, and afterward training the combined model on image-text data. In contrast, natively multi-modal models are pretrained jointly on text, images, and often audio and video, from the start of training. The developers of several frontier models describe them as natively multi-modal: Google describes the Gemini models as multi-modal from the ground up, trained jointly across text, image, audio, and video data [21]; OpenAI described GPT-4o as a single model trained end-to-end across text, vision, and audio [22]; and Meta describes Llama 4 as natively multi-modal, using early fusion to integrate text and vision tokens into a unified model backbone [17].

Note that these terms are not used consistently. For instance, Llama 4 still uses a vision encoder based on MetaCLIP, so in this case, “early fusion” refers mainly to joint pretraining of a single backbone on text and visual tokens, rather than to the absence of a vision encoder. A recent study that trained native multi-modal models from scratch found no inherent advantage of late fusion over early fusion; early-fusion models performed better at smaller model sizes and were more efficient to train [23].

Frontier models. The exact architectures of commercial frontier models, such as the Gemini, GPT, and Claude families of models, are not publicly disclosed. Therefore, although their developers describe joint multi-modal training, we cannot tell which fusion design or connector they use. Meanwhile, many open-weight VLMs are widely accessible to the research community, including Qwen-VL, Llama Vision, InternVL, Pixtral, and others.

22.3 Vision Encoders and Training Objectives

This section describes how vision encoders convert images into visual tokens, and the objectives used to train VLMs.

22.3.1 Vision Encoders in VLMs

As explained in the section on Vision Transformers in Lecture 19, a ViT divides the image into patches, embeds each patch, and uses self-attention to allow the patch representations to incorporate information from other parts of the image. Positional information helps the model represent where the features occur. For example, a 224 × 224 image divided into 16 × 16 patches produces 14 × 14 = 196 patch positions. If a projector maps each feature to an embedding vector with dimension 4,096, the resulting sequence of visual tokens has shape 196 × 4,096.

Higher-resolution images can preserve small text and fine details, but they generally produce more visual tokens and increase the memory use and computation. To manage the number of visual tokens, models may resize images, divide them into tiles, merge neighboring features, or use learned resampling.

For instance, Qwen3-VL uses 16 × 16 spatial patches and merges 2 × 2 neighboring features, producing a spatial compression factor of 32. A 512 × 512 image therefore yields approximately (512 / 32)² = 16² = 256 visual tokens [13, 14].

However, for document analysis or medical images, aggressive resizing can remove exactly the small features that are needed to answer a question.

22.3.2 Training Objectives

Contrastive learning: CLIP learns to assign higher similarity to matched image-text pairs than to mismatched pairs within a training batch. It applies classification losses in both the image-to-text and text-to-image directions, and its output is an embedding used for matching images with text [9].

Conditional next-token prediction: a generative VLM predicts the next token based on the image, the user prompt, and the preceding response tokens. During supervised training, the preceding tokens come from the reference response, and during inference, they come from the model’s own generated output. The loss is typically computed only on the tokens of the response, while the image and prompt tokens serve as the input.

Instruction tuning and post-training: Caption pretraining teaches associations between images and descriptions. Instruction tuning uses examples such as questions about images, explanations, and structured extraction tasks to teach the model useful response behavior. Some models also undergo preference optimization or reinforcement learning to improve response quality and reasoning.

Training stages: A common training strategy for VLMs with projected visual tokens involves multiple stages, as shown in the next figure. In the first stage, only the multi-modal projector (also referred to as fusion layer, adapter, or connector) is trained, while the image encoder and the LLM text decoder are kept frozen. This is followed by a second, instruction-tuning stage that involves additional finetuning of the multi-modal projector and parts of the LLM text decoder, while the image encoder is kept frozen. Other training recipes also finetune the vision encoder, or add further post-training stages.

Figure: VLM training. Source: [4].

22.4 Finetuning Example: Qwen3-VL on Radiology Images

This section presents an example of finetuning a VLM on a custom dataset containing medical images, including X-rays, CT scans, and ultrasound images that show various medical conditions and diseases. Each image is paired with a caption from the published article in which the image appeared. The dataset was created by the Unsloth team [8], and it is a smaller sampled version of the ROCO (Radiology Objects in COntext) dataset.

The goal is to adapt a general-purpose VLM, which does not have in-depth knowledge of medical image analysis, to the terminology and style of radiology image descriptions.

By relying on parameter-efficient finetuning techniques, including quantization and LoRA, we will finetune the VLM using a single L4 GPU.

Data Collator

The next cell defines a data collator for Qwen3-VL that combines images and text into batches for processing by the VLM. The collator also creates the labels for training. The tokens of the user prompt (including the image tokens) and the padding tokens are masked by assigning them the value -100, so that the loss is calculated only on the tokens of the assistant’s response. The final <|im_end|> token of the response is not masked, so that the model learns when to stop generating text.

Note that the processor expands the single <|image_pad|> placeholder in the text into many visual tokens (256 to 512 for our image budget). Therefore, the start of the assistant’s response is found by searching the processed input IDs, rather than by tokenizing the prompt text separately.

# Collate text and images into batches for Qwen3VLM
# The code in this cell is not required in preparation for quizzes
class Qwen3VLDataCollator:
    def __init__(self, processor):
        self.processor = processor
        # Token IDs of the assistant header that precedes each response
        self.assistant_ids = processor.tokenizer.encode("<|im_start|>assistant\n", add_special_tokens=False)

    def find_response_start(self, input_ids):
        # Search the processed input IDs, where each image placeholder has already been
        # expanded into many visual tokens, for the assistant header
        n = len(self.assistant_ids)
        for i in range(len(input_ids) - n + 1):
            if input_ids[i:i + n] == self.assistant_ids:
                return i + n
        raise ValueError("Assistant header not found in the sample.")

    def __call__(self, features):
        # Extract text and images
        batch_texts = [f["text"] for f in features]
        batch_images = [f["image"] for f in features]

        # Process
        batch = self.processor(text=batch_texts, images=batch_images, padding=True, truncation=False, return_tensors="pt")

        # Create labels: the loss is calculated only on the assistant's response and its final <|im_end|> token
        labels = batch["input_ids"].clone()
        for idx in range(len(features)):
            start = self.find_response_start(batch["input_ids"][idx].tolist())
            # Mask the user prompt, the image tokens, and the assistant header
            labels[idx, :start] = -100

        # Mask the padding tokens
        labels[batch["attention_mask"] == 0] = -100

        batch["labels"] = labels

        return batch
# Apply the collator class
data_collator = Qwen3VLDataCollator(processor)

# Check the training targets for one sample: only the caption and <|im_end|> should be printed
check_batch = data_collator([train_dataset[0]])
target_ids = check_batch["labels"][0]
print(processor.tokenizer.decode(target_ids[target_ids != -100]))

Test the VLM Responses Before Finetuning

Let’s check the responses by Qwen3-VL for three images from the held-out evaluation subset before finetuning the model. We use greedy decoding (do_sample=False), so that the responses are deterministic, and the responses before and after finetuning can be compared fairly.

Notice in the output below that the model correctly identifies the type of each image (a panoramic dental radiograph, an angiogram, and an ultrasound image) and describes the general anatomy. However, the descriptions are generic, rather than focused on the medical findings, and they also contain errors. For instance, the model describes the third image as an obstetric examination, whereas the ground truth caption describes the endometrium, and it guesses the meaning of the on-screen labels. In addition, all three responses are cut off because of the limit of 128 new tokens.

# The code in this cell is not required in preparation for quizzes
instruction = "Describe this medical image in detail."

# Test with 3 samples from the held-out evaluation subset
test_indices = [0, 5, 10]

for idx in test_indices:

    print(f"TEST EXAMPLE {idx}")

    test_image = dataset[idx]["image"]
    ground_truth = dataset[idx]["caption"]

    # Show image
    display_image = test_image.copy()
    display_image.thumbnail((300, 300))
    display(display_image)

    # Format input
    messages = [{"role": "user", "content": [{"type": "image", "image": test_image}, {"type": "text", "text": instruction}]}]

    # Apply chat template
    text = processor.apply_chat_template(messages, tokenize=False, add_generation_prompt=True)

    # Process inputs
    inputs = processor(text=[text], images=[test_image], padding=True, return_tensors="pt").to(model.device)

    print("Generated Caption: ")
    with torch.inference_mode():
        # Greedy decoding (do_sample=False) makes the responses before and after finetuning comparable
        output = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=128, do_sample=False,
            repetition_penalty=1.2, use_cache=True,
            pad_token_id=processor.tokenizer.pad_token_id,
            eos_token_id=processor.tokenizer.eos_token_id)

    # Decode only the newly generated tokens (the assistant's response)
    response = processor.decode(output[0][inputs["input_ids"].shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True)

    print(response)
    print("\nGround Truth:")
    print(ground_truth)

Finetuning the model took about 20 minutes on an L4 GPU, where each of the 40 steps used 8 × 4 = 32 samples (a batch size of 8 with 4 gradient accumulation steps), i.e., a total of 1,280 samples out of the 1,878 available samples in the training dataset were used (0.68 epochs). As shown in the training log below, the training loss decreases quickly from about 4.2 to about 2.2 within the first 12 steps, and afterward it levels off. If you run out of GPU memory, reduce the batch size and increase the gradient accumulation steps accordingly (e.g., 4 × 8 or 2 × 16), so that the effective batch size remains 32. Training on a T4 GPU would take longer, and it would require changing the precision from the currently used bfloat16 to float16 (i.e., bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.float16 and dtype=torch.float16 when loading the model, and fp16=True instead of bf16=True in SFTConfig), because bfloat16 is not supported by T4 GPUs.

Note that the responses generated by the finetuned VLM are much shorter than the responses before finetuning, and they follow the concise style and clinical vocabulary of the captions in the finetuning dataset. The responses also end properly, because the model learned to generate the <|im_end|> token at the end of a response.

However, the findings in the responses mostly do not match the ground truth captions. For the first image, the model correctly mentions a lesion indicated by arrows, but places it in the mandible rather than in the maxilla; for the second image, it describes a LIMA graft, but misses the compression and flow interruption; and for the third image, it incorrectly describes a gestational sac. The responses also contain invented details that cannot be inferred from the images, such as the patient’s age and the date of the examination. This is because the captions in the dataset were taken from published case reports, and the model learned to imitate their narrative style. In other words, using more clinical terminology does not mean that the model’s visual understanding of the images has improved.

These hallucinated clinical details illustrate why the outputs of a finetuned VLM must be evaluated on many held-out examples and validated by clinicians before such a model can be used as a tool by medical professionals.

22.5 Benchmarking VLMs

The performance of VLMs is assessed using multi-modal benchmarks that evaluate models on a variety of tasks, such as reasoning, visual question answering, document comprehension, and video understanding. Most benchmarks consist of a set of images with associated questions, often posed as multiple-choice questions. Popular benchmarks include MMMU, Video-MME, MathVista, and ChartQA. MMMU is one of the most comprehensive benchmarks, and contains 11.5K multi-modal questions that require knowledge and reasoning across different disciplines, such as art and engineering.

Several VLM-specific leaderboards provide comparative rankings across diverse metrics. Vision Arena ranks models based on anonymous votes by human users who compare the outputs of two models. The Open VLM Leaderboard ranks VLMs according to their scores on different benchmarks and their average scores. Note that human preference and factual accuracy measure different things, and benchmark scores do not guarantee good performance on a specific application.

22.6 Importance of VLMs

Traditional computer vision (CV) models are constrained to learning from a predefined and fixed set of categories or objects for image classification or object detection (e.g., identifying whether an image contains a cat or a dog). Moreover, these tasks require users to manually label a large number of images with a specific category for classification, or to draw bounding boxes around multiple objects in each image for object detection, which is a tedious, time-consuming, and expensive process.

Conversely, VLMs are trained with more detailed textual descriptions of images, where, for example, an image can contain cats, dogs, and other objects, and the text description can provide additional contextual information (e.g., the cat is sitting, the dog is running, etc.). Learning from rich natural language descriptions allows VLMs to better understand visual scenes, without limiting the learning to a narrow set of visual concepts comprising a fixed number of classes and objects. It also greatly reduces the need for manual image labeling, and extends the utility of VLMs beyond traditional CV tasks like classification or detection to new capabilities, including reasoning, summarization, question answering, and interactive dialogue, by simply changing the text prompt.

VLMs have been applied across many domains and industries, and offer great potential to enhance visual perception [6]. For instance, they can be used to review videos and extract insights for industrial inspection and robotics (detect faults, monitor operations, identify anomalies), safety and infrastructure monitoring (recognize floods, fires, or traffic hazards), retail and logistics (track empty shelves, detect misplaced items, identify supply-chain bottlenecks), and numerous other tasks. Whether a VLM is accurate and fast enough for a particular application, however, needs to be evaluated for that application.

22.7 VLM Limitations

VLM responses can sound confident even when the visual evidence is weak. Important limitations of VLMs include:

  • Hallucinations: inventing objects, findings, or contextual details that are not supported by the input.
  • Language shortcuts: answering based on plausible textual associations instead of the actual image.
  • Fine-detail errors: missing small text, subtle findings, or features that were removed by resizing.
  • Counting and spatial errors: confusing object counts, positions, relationships, or orientation.
  • Temporal errors: missing a short event or incorrectly ordering events in sampled video frames.
  • Domain shift: failing on image types, devices, or settings that differ from the training data.

Several of these limitations, in particular hallucinated details, can be observed in the finetuning example in Section 22.4.

22.8 Other Multi-modal Models

Many video-language models sample frames from a video and encode them into visual tokens. Frame order, timestamps, and temporal positional information help represent events over time. Sampling more frames can capture brief events but increases the number of input tokens, whereas sparse sampling can miss such events. For example, Qwen3-VL supports video inputs and explicit timestamp-based temporal alignment [13, 14].

Audio-capable models can encode speech or other sounds and produce text, speech, or both. Direct audio processing can preserve cues such as speaker identity, emotion, and intonation that are lost when only a transcript is supplied. Qwen3-Omni is an example of a model that processes text, images, audio, and video, and generates text and speech [15].

Generative models for visual generation and editing can create or edit images and videos. Visual generation often relies on diffusion or flow-matching components, rather than only on a text decoder. Some systems, such as UniVideo, combine visual understanding, generation, and editing in a single model [16].

Visual AI agents can interpret screenshots and supply actions to a tool that operates a user interface. Vision-language-action (VLA) models extend this idea to physical actions. For instance, in robotics, such systems can use a camera to capture images of the environment, a VLM to interpret the images and propose actions, and an action module to control the robot’s movements while executing the actions.
